Radical & Complex Simplifier

The imaginary unit $i$ is defined by $i=\sqrt{-1}$, so $i^2=-1$. For a negative radicand, $\sqrt{-n}=\sqrt{n}\cdot\sqrt{-1}=i\sqrt{n}$ for $n>0$. A complex number $a+bi$ pairs a real part $a$ with an imaginary part $bi$; it plots as a point on the complex plane, real axis horizontal, imaginary axis vertical — the same grid the quadratic analyzer uses when the discriminant goes negative.

Simplifying $\sqrt{212}$ means pulling the largest perfect-square factor out from under the radical: write $212$ as (perfect square)×(remainder), take the square root of the square factor, and leave the remainder inside.

FACTOR TREE — √212
4 × 53 = 212
→ 2√53

Quadratic Analyzer

For $f(x)=ax^2+bx+c$, the vertex is the turning point $(h,k)$ where $h=-\dfrac{b}{2a}$ and $k=f(h)$. Writing the function as $f(x)=a(x-h)^2+k$ — vertex form — makes the roles explicit: $h$ sits inside the parenthesis and shifts the graph horizontally (blue), while $k$ sits outside and shifts it vertically (red). The axis of symmetry is the vertical line $x=h$. If $a>0$ the parabola opens up and $k$ is a minimum; if $a<0$ it opens down and $k$ is a maximum.

Whether the roots are real traces back to the discriminant, $D=b^2-4ac$, computed inside the exact solver.

Exact Quadratic Solver

The square root property says: if $(x-p)^2=q$, then $x-p=\pm\sqrt{q}$. Completing the square turns any $x^2+bx=d$ into that shape by adding $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$ to both sides, the amount that makes the left side a perfect square trinomial.

The discriminant $D=b^2-4ac$ (used already in the quadratic analyzer) predicts the outcome before you solve: $D>0$ gives two real roots, $D=0$ gives one, and $D<0$ gives a complex conjugate pair.

Mechanism — step through completing the square, x²+x+3=0
Step 0 of 4 — press Next to begin.
Routine logic
flowchart TD
A[Prompt A,B,C] --> B["D = B² - 4AC"]
B --> C{D sign}
C -- "D ≥ 0" --> D["x = (-B ± √D) / (2A), simplify radical"]
C -- "D < 0" --> E["x = (-B ± i√|D|) / (2A)"]
D --> F[Disp exact roots]
E --> F

Vertex / Zeros Reconstructor

A parabola is fully determined by three numbers: a vertex $(h,k)$ and one more point $(x_1,y_1)$ it passes through. Substitute both into vertex form $f(x)=a(x-h)^2+k$ and solve for the one unknown, $a$, which fixes how narrow or wide the graph is and whether it reflects. This is the reverse of the quadratic analyzer: that routine starts from $a,b,c$ and finds $(h,k)$; this one starts from $(h,k)$ and a point and finds $a$.

The same two-unknowns-one-point idea works for any function with one well-defined key point, not just a parabola — see program B, the parent-function transformer. It breaks down for a cubic or quartic with several turning points, since no single point plays the role $(h,k)$ plays here; those are handled instead by program C, which anchors on zeros rather than a vertex.

Routine logic
flowchart TD
A[Prompt h,k,x1,y1] --> B["solve y1 = a(x1-h)² + k for a"]
B --> C["f(x) = a(x-h)² + k"]
C --> D[Disp vertex-form equation]

Synthetic & Long Division

Synthetic division divides a polynomial by a linear factor $x-k$ using only the coefficients. Bring down the leading coefficient, then repeatedly multiply the last number written by $k$ and add it into the next column. The bottom row reads as the quotient's coefficients (one degree lower) with the last entry as the remainder. A remainder of $0$ means $x-k$ is a factor — the same fact the polynomial analyzer uses to confirm a zero.

Mechanism — reveal the synthetic division table column by column
Routine logic
flowchart TD
A[Enter coeff list, k] --> B[Bring down leading coeff]
B --> C[Multiply by k]
C --> D[Add into next column]
D --> E{more columns?}
E -- yes --> C
E -- no --> F[Last entry = remainder; rest = quotient]

Polynomial Zero Analyzer

For a factored polynomial, each factor $(x-r)^p$ contributes a zero at $x=r$ with multiplicity $p$: odd multiplicity crosses the x-axis, even multiplicity touches and turns back — the same rule the sign-chart solver relies on to decide whether a region's sign flips. The end behavior is set by the degree (sum of all multiplicities) and the leading coefficient: even degree means both ends point the same way, odd degree means opposite ways; a positive leading coefficient rises right, negative falls right.

This routine reads zeros off an already-factored $f(x)$. Running it backward — zeros in, factored form out — is program C. Handed one zero $k$ instead of the whole factored form, program D divides it out and solves for the rest.

Routine logic
flowchart TD
A[Read factored form] --> B[Sum exponents = degree]
B --> C{degree even or odd?}
C -- even --> D{leading sign}
C -- odd --> E{leading sign}
D -- "+" --> F[rises both ends]
D -- "-" --> G[falls both ends]
E -- "+" --> H[falls left, rises right]
E -- "-" --> I[rises left, falls right]
F & G & H & I --> J[Disp zeros + multiplicities + end behavior]

Rational Function Analyzer

For $f(x)=\dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$, the domain excludes every zero of $q(x)$. A vertical asymptote occurs at an excluded value that is not also a zero of $p(x)$; if it is a shared zero, the graph instead has a hole there — a single removable point the rational equation solver calls an extraneous solution when it shows up algebraically. The horizontal asymptote compares the degrees of $p$ and $q$: equal degrees give $y=$ (ratio of leading coefficients).

B --> C{shared zero?}
C -- yes --> D[hole at that x]
C -- no --> E[vertical asymptote there]
D & E --> F[Compare deg p, deg q for HA]
F --> G[Disp domain, intercepts, VA, HA, holes]

The routine finds every root of the cleared equation, then substitutes each one back into the original denominators before accepting it.

Sign-Chart Inequality Solver

A polynomial or rational inequality is solved with a sign chart: mark every critical value (zero of the numerator or denominator) on a number line, which splits it into regions; test one point in each region to find the sign of the whole expression there. A critical value from an odd-multiplicity factor flips the sign crossing it; an even-multiplicity factor does not — the same odd/even rule used in the polynomial analyzer. Only a numerator zero can close an endpoint with $\le$ or $\ge$; a denominator zero is always excluded, open.

Mechanism — reveal each region's sign for −4u(u+2)²(5−u) > 0
Regions: (−∞,−2), (−2,0), (0,5), (5,∞). Press to test each.

B --> C[Test a point in each region]

D -- no --> F[exclude region]

Parent Function Transformer

A parent function is the simplest member of a family of curves: $y=x$, $y=|x|$, $y=x^2$, $y=\sqrt{x}$, $y=x^3$, $y=1/x$. Every other member of that family is a transformation of the parent, $f(x)=a\cdot\text{parent}(x-h)+k$. $h$ sits inside the parenthesis and shifts the graph horizontally (blue); $k$ sits outside and shifts it vertically (red); $a$ stretches, compresses, or reflects the shape (ochre). This is exactly the vertex-form idea from program 2, generalized past parabolas.

Give the routine a known point $(h,k)$ — the vertex for quadratic or absolute value, the inflection point for a cubic, the center for a reciprocal, or any point at all for a line — plus one more point $(x_1,y_1)$ the curve passes through, and it solves $a=\dfrac{y_1-k}{\text{parent}(x_1-h)}$.

Mechanism — pick a type, enter the two points
Routine logic

B --> C[Prompt second point x1,y1]
C --> D["a = (y1-k) / parent(x1-h)"]
D --> E{parent(x1-h) defined?}
E -- no --> F[Disp domain error, request new point]
E -- yes --> G["f(x) = a·parent(x-h) + k"]
G --> H[Disp translation sentence]

Zeros + Point → Factored Form

A cubic, a quartic, or anything beyond a parabola can have several turning points, so there's no single $(h,k)$ for program B to anchor on. What stays well-defined at any degree is the set of zeros and their multiplicities, read straight off the x-intercepts of a graph. Knowing them fixes $f(x)=a(x-r_1)^{m_1}(x-r_2)^{m_2}\cdots$ up to the one remaining unknown, the leading coefficient $a$ — any nonzero multiple of a factored polynomial has the exact same zeros, so zeros alone can't pin down $a$. One more point off the x-axis, often the y-intercept, does: substitute it and solve.

Zero-Chain Solver

Given one zero $k$ of a polynomial, synthetic division by $(x-k)$ peels it off and leaves a quotient one degree lower, with remainder $0$ confirming $k$ really is a zero. If that quotient is quadratic, hand it to the exact solver — real or complex roots both fall out the same way. Chaining these two routines turns "one zero is $k$" into the complete zero list or the complete factored form, whichever the problem asks for.
